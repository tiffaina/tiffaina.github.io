What 'Accountability' Actually Means When Building Medical AI

February 2026 • 7 min read

Let's take this hypothetical: I'm sitting in a radiology reading room at AP-HP Pitié-Salpêtrière, watching a radiologist scroll through spine CT scans. She pauses on one, points to a vertebra that looks slightly off to me, and says: "Transitional anomaly. The model missed it."

This is the moment where accountability stops being a buzzword and becomes a real problem. The foundation model I'm working with flagged dozens of normal variations as anomalies, but missed this one—a case where clinical intervention might actually matter. So who's responsible? Me, for how I fine-tuned it? The team that trained the foundation model? The hospital that deployed it? The radiologist who trusted it?

The answer is: it's complicated. And that's the problem.

The Accountability Vacuum

Healthcare has always had clear accountability structures. When something goes wrong, there's a chain of responsibility: the attending physician, the resident, the nurse, the hospital system. Everyone knows who answers for what. Medical malpractice law, licensing boards, hospital review committees—these exist because when you're dealing with human lives, someone needs to be accountable.

AI breaks this. Not because the technology is magic, but because it fragments decision-making across so many actors that traditional accountability structures don't know where to land.

Consider the typical pipeline for a medical AI model reaching a patient:

  • Researchers publish a foundation model trained on millions of images
  • A company fine-tunes it for a specific clinical task
  • A hospital licenses and deploys it in their radiology workflow
  • A radiologist uses its outputs to inform their diagnosis
  • A patient receives treatment based on that diagnosis

If the model is wrong and the patient is harmed, who's liable? The researchers who trained the base model can say "we published limitations and never claimed clinical validity." The company can say "we validated on our test set and performance was within acceptable bounds." The hospital can say "our radiologists have final say." The radiologist can say "the model was FDA-cleared and I trusted it."

Everyone has a defensible position. No one is clearly responsible.

What Makes Medical AI Different

You might be thinking: isn't this just like any other medical device? If an MRI machine malfunctions, we have protocols. If a drug has unforeseen side effects, we have reporting systems. Why should AI be different?

The difference is opacity and adaptability. An MRI machine is a static piece of hardware with predictable failure modes. A drug's mechanism of action can be studied and understood. But a deep learning model trained on millions of images is fundamentally different from either—it's making decisions based on patterns that even its creators can't fully articulate, and those patterns can shift as data distributions change.

When I'm working on spine morphometrics, I can tell you that my model looks at vertebral shape, spacing, alignment. But I can't tell you exactly why it flagged a particular scan as anomalous. The features it learned are distributed across millions of parameters. There's no single decision rule I can point to and say "here's where it went wrong."

This opacity matters for accountability because you can't fix what you can't see. If a radiologist makes a diagnostic error, you can review their reasoning, identify where the logic broke down, and provide targeted training. If a model makes an error, you retrain on more data and hope the problem doesn't recur—but you don't really know why it happened or if your fix addressed the root cause.

The Explainability Trap

The standard response to this opacity problem is: make the models explainable. Show saliency maps. Generate attention visualizations. Provide feature importance scores. Let clinicians "see" what the model is looking at.

I'm skeptical of this as a solution to accountability. Not because explainability isn't valuable—it is. But because what counts as an "explanation" in machine learning research is often not what counts as an explanation in clinical practice.

A saliency map showing that my model paid attention to L5-S1 region when flagging an anomaly is interesting. But it doesn't tell a radiologist what pattern the model saw or whether that pattern is clinically meaningful. It's like showing someone a heatmap of where you looked while reading a medical chart and claiming that explains your diagnosis. The where doesn't capture the why.

Explainability techniques give us the illusion of understanding without the substance. And that illusion might be more dangerous than admitting we don't know.

Real clinical accountability requires understanding failure modes—not just seeing where the model looked, but knowing what specific clinical scenarios it's likely to miss, what types of edge cases break it, what happens when imaging quality degrades. That level of understanding requires extensive prospective validation, not just attention weights.

What Accountability Could Look Like

So if current accountability structures don't work and explainability isn't a silver bullet, what does responsible deployment of medical AI actually require?

I think it starts with being honest about uncertainty. Models should come with not just accuracy metrics but detailed failure mode documentation. Here's what our model is good at. Here's where it breaks. Here's how performance degrades with different scanners, patient populations, image quality levels. Here are the cases where you absolutely cannot trust it.

It also requires continuous monitoring in deployment. Medical AI shouldn't be "cleared" once and then forgotten. Performance should be tracked over time, with clear thresholds for when a model needs to be retrained or retired. When data distributions shift—new scanner protocols, different patient demographics, evolving clinical practices—we need systems to detect that drift before it leads to harm.

And critically, accountability needs to flow upstream. If a company deploys a model that fails in predictable ways that they should have caught in validation, they should be liable. If a hospital deploys a model without proper integration into clinical workflows or clinician training, they should be liable. If a researcher publishes a model with known limitations that are buried in supplementary materials while promoting clinical applications, they should be liable.

This isn't about stifling innovation. It's about making sure that the people building and deploying these systems have real skin in the game when things go wrong.

Living in the Uncertainty

The thing I keep coming back to: we're building these systems now, deploying them now, affecting patient care now—all while the accountability structures are still being figured out.

That radiologist who found the anomaly my model missed? She didn't stop using the model. She just learned to trust it less for that specific type of case. Over time, she's building her own mental model of what the AI is good for and where human judgment needs to override it. This is adaptation, and it's happening in hospitals everywhere.

But individual adaptation isn't the same as systematic accountability. We need structures that work at scale, that protect patients even when individual clinicians haven't yet learned a model's quirks through trial and error.

I don't have a neat answer for how to build those structures. What I do know is that we can't keep pretending the old frameworks will work, or that technical solutions like explainability will paper over the fundamental problem. Accountability in medical AI is a sociotechnical challenge, and it's going to require more than just better algorithms.

It's going to require us to be honest about what we don't know, what we can't predict, and who should answer when things go wrong. And right now, we're not even having that conversation seriously enough.
